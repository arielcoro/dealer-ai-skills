# Dealer Store Positioning — Reference Framework

The positioning architecture, differentiation methodology, and quality bar. SKILL.md references this file. Read it before generating any positioning brief.

---

## The positioning architecture

Every dealer positioning brief follows the same four-layer architecture. Each layer answers a specific question and feeds the layer below.

### Layer 1. Target customer

**Question answered:** Who specifically is this dealership for?

Not everyone. Not "anyone in the market who needs a car." Specific customers with specific buying motivations who specifically benefit from what this dealership offers.

Dealer positioning fails most often at this layer because dealers refuse to pick. They want everyone. The result is messaging that means nothing to anyone.

**Quality bar for this layer:**
- 2 to 3 customer segments named
- Each segment has demographic and psychographic specifics
- Each segment has a clear buying motivation that the dealership uniquely serves
- The segments together cover the dealership's actual revenue base (not aspirational customers)

### Layer 2. Value proposition

**Question answered:** What does this dealership uniquely deliver to those customers?

A single sentence. Plain language. No jargon. The thing the dealership provides that the customer values and that the dealership delivers consistently.

**Quality bar for this layer:**
- One sentence (15 to 30 words)
- Specific verb (not "provide" or "offer")
- Tangible outcome
- True today, not aspirational
- Could not be said equally well by the competitor down the street

### Layer 3. Differentiation

**Question answered:** Why should the customer pick this dealership over the competitor?

Three to five specific, defensible facts. Each backed by evidence. No generic "we care more" claims.

A claim is defensible when it relies on one of:
- A fact competitors cannot quickly copy (OEM award, history, location, capability)
- A process discipline competitors do not have (specific service standard, BDC response time, F&I transparency standard)
- A relationship asset competitors do not have (long-tenured staff, OEM master technician count, specific community involvement)

**Quality bar for this layer:**
- 3 to 5 differentiators
- Each has supporting evidence
- Each is concrete and specific
- Each is current (not "we won this in 2008")
- No unsubstantiated superlatives

### Layer 4. Voice, tone, and brand promise

**Question answered:** How does this dealership consistently show up?

- **Voice:** 3 to 5 specific adjectives that describe how the brand sounds.
- **Tone variations:** how voice shifts across contexts (service vs sales vs community).
- **Brand promise:** the single "we will always" statement.

**Quality bar for this layer:**
- Voice adjectives are specific and behavioral (not generic like "professional")
- Tone variations are real, not abstract
- Brand promise is operational, not aspirational
- Examples of on-brand and off-brand phrasing are concrete

## The differentiation methodology

Finding the defensible positioning angle is the hardest part of dealer positioning. Most dealers default to claims that competitors can easily make: "we treat customers like family," "we have great service," "we have low prices." These claims do not produce differentiation because every dealer says them.

The methodology that works:

### Step 1. Audit the structural advantages

What does the dealership have that competitors cannot quickly replicate?

- **Location.** Distance to major commuter corridors, distance to suburban concentrations, proximity to military base or university.
- **History.** Years in business, family ownership, founding story.
- **OEM relationships.** Mark of Excellence, President's Award, OEM specialization (e.g., RAM Towing Center certification).
- **Specialization.** Master technician count, specific capability (off-road conversions, EV charging install, fleet service).
- **Bilingual capability.** Spanish-speaking staff across departments, Hispanic community involvement.
- **Inventory position.** Largest in metro for specific brand, exclusive franchise for specific model.

### Step 2. Audit the process disciplines

What does the dealership do operationally that competitors are inconsistent on?

- BDC response time discipline (e.g., always under 15 minutes during business hours).
- Service communication discipline (e.g., daily customer updates for multi-day repairs).
- F&I transparency (e.g., menu presentation with no add-on pressure).
- Trade transparency (e.g., walk-through of the trade evaluation).
- Service follow-up (e.g., 7-day post-visit call).

These often emerge from analyzing positive review patterns (use dealer-customer-sentiment-analyzer output as input).

### Step 3. Audit the relationship assets

What human or community connections does the dealership have that competitors lack?

- Long-tenured staff (the sales manager who has been there 22 years and knows the third generation of buying families).
- Community involvement (named programs, sponsorships, philanthropy).
- Press relationships (regular coverage in local press).
- OEM specialty relationships (district manager closeness, regional OEM events hosted).

### Step 4. Test against competitor claims

For each candidate differentiator, ask: would [Top Competitor] be able to claim this with a straight face? If yes, it is not differentiation. If no, it is.

Real differentiation passes this test. Generic claims fail it.

### Step 5. Validate with customer evidence

The strongest differentiation lines up with what customers actually praise. Cross-reference candidate differentiators against:
- Recurring positive review themes
- Named-staff praise patterns
- "Why customers come back" patterns the GM can observe
- Word-of-mouth referral patterns

If the candidate differentiator does not show up in customer evidence, it may be a self-perception that customers do not actually experience.

## The "we are / we are not" framework

A two-column boundary list that prevents brand drift over time.

**"We are" column:**
- 8 to 12 specific positioning boundaries the dealership lives by

**"We are not" column:**
- 8 to 12 specific things the dealership deliberately is not (positions the dealership does not pursue)

This list does the heavy lifting when the marketing team is improvising. When a junior marketer asks "should we run this messaging?", the "we are not" list answers most of the questions.

Examples of "we are" entries:
- We are direct about pricing
- We are family-owned (or group-owned with family-feel)
- We are a no-pressure F&I experience
- We are the dealership that follows up after service
- We are bilingual in sales, service, and F&I

Examples of "we are not" entries:
- We are not a high-pressure showroom
- We are not the cheapest dealer (we are the most transparent)
- We are not chasing one-time deals at the expense of the second sale
- We are not afraid to send a customer to a different dealer if it's better for them
- We are not making promises in marketing that the showroom cannot keep

The "we are not" entries are more diagnostic than the "we are" entries because they show what the dealership has deliberately decided to give up. Every positioning involves trade-offs.

## How positioning feeds other marketing skills

The positioning brief is foundational input for downstream skills:

- **dealer-aeo-content-brief.** Pulls the voice, target customer, and value props to brief content writers.
- **dealer-email-flows.** Uses the voice and tone variations for email copy.
- **dealer-new-customer-onboarding.** Uses the brand promise and "we are" list for the onboarding experience design.
- **dealer-comparison-page-builder.** Uses the differentiation list to structure comparison content.
- **dealer-customer-sentiment-analyzer.** Validates whether the positioning is actually being delivered (positive reviews should align with the differentiation; negative reviews against the brand promise are operational signals).
- **dealer-call-transcript-classifier.** The voice and tone variations inform what "good handling" sounds like.
- **dealer-vdp-merchandising-review.** The voice influences VDP description tone.

When the positioning brief changes (typically every 12 to 24 months), the marketing director should re-run the downstream skills with the updated positioning as input.

## Quality bar for the final document

Before delivering the positioning brief, verify:

- [ ] Target customer is 2 to 3 specific segments, not "anyone."
- [ ] Value proposition is one sentence, plain language, true today.
- [ ] Differentiation is 3 to 5 defensible facts with evidence.
- [ ] Voice is 3 to 5 specific behavioral adjectives.
- [ ] Brand promise is operational, not aspirational.
- [ ] "We are / we are not" lists have 8 to 12 specific items each.
- [ ] Competitor positioning is named and analyzed.
- [ ] No unsubstantiated superlatives in any section.
- [ ] No fabricated facts; placeholders marked `[CONFIRM]` where needed.
- [ ] Spanish version produced (if bilingual market).

If any check fails, fix the brief before output.

## Common positioning failure modes

The five most common ways dealer positioning fails. Every brief should explicitly prevent these.

**1. Trying to be for everyone.** "Our target customer is anyone who needs a car." This produces messaging that means nothing to anyone. Fix: pick specific segments, name them, give them specifics.

**2. Generic differentiation.** "We have great service, friendly staff, and competitive prices." Every dealer says this. Fix: defensible differentiation methodology (Steps 1-5 above).

**3. Slogans instead of positioning.** "Drive happy with [Dealership]." A slogan is not positioning. Positioning is the architecture that produces slogans, not the slogan itself. Fix: insist on the architecture (target customer → value prop → differentiation → voice).

**4. Aspirational instead of operational.** "We always go above and beyond." If the operations do not consistently deliver this, the promise breaks trust. Fix: positioning reflects what the dealership actually does today.

**5. Annual rewrite chasing competitor messaging.** Some dealers refresh positioning every time a competitor makes a move. This destroys consistency and trains the team that messaging is disposable. Fix: positioning reviewed annually with a high bar for change.
