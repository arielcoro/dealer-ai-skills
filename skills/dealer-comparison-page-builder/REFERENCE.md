# Dealer Comparison Page Builder — Reference Framework

The comparison page architecture, fair-play rules, and the 10-to-15 dimension comparison framework. SKILL.md references this file. Read it before producing any comparison page.

---

## Why dealer comparison pages work

When a buyer is genuinely shopping two dealerships, they often search "[Dealer A] vs [Dealer B]" or ask an AI engine "should I go to [Dealer A] or [Dealer B]." Most dealers produce no content for this query. The buyer ends up at a third-party site, a forum, or a Reddit thread with limited information.

The dealer that publishes a fair, honest comparison page wins on two surfaces:

1. **Google search.** Ranks for "[Dealer A] vs [Dealer B]" because no one else has the page.
2. **AI engines.** Gets cited when the buyer asks a comparison question because the page provides extractable comparative answers.

Plus a third benefit, often overlooked:

3. **Sales floor confidence.** The salesperson who has read the comparison page can navigate cross-shopping conversations with respect and specifics, not with disparagement or hedging.

---

## The fair-play rules

These are not optional. They are the difference between a page that performs and a page that backfires.

### Rule 1. The competitor must come out ahead in at least one area

A comparison page that claims this dealer wins on every dimension is not credible. Buyers see through it. AI engines deprioritize it because it reads as marketing rather than information. The competitor's GM, if they read it, would dismiss it as fluff.

A fair page acknowledges at least one of:
- A dimension where the competitor is genuinely stronger
- A scenario where the competitor is the better recommendation
- A real strength of the competitor (volume, history, specific capability, OEM relationship)

The acknowledgment can be specific and limited ("Birmingham Buick has been in the metro 15 years longer and has stronger long-term parts inventory for older GMs") without conceding the overall recommendation.

### Rule 2. All claims about the competitor must be verifiable

Every fact about the competitor in the comparison page must come from:
- The competitor's public website
- Their Google Business Profile
- Their OEM dealer locator listing
- Public press, social media, or trade coverage
- Public review data on Google, DealerRater, Cars.com

The competitor's marketing director should be able to read the page and not find a single false claim. Honest comparison beats inflated comparison every time.

### Rule 3. No disparagement

Even when the user has negative impressions of the competitor, the page does not use disparaging language. The line is: factual statements about specific weaknesses are fine; characterizations of the competitor as bad, untrustworthy, or low-quality are not.

Acceptable: "Birmingham Buick does not currently publish service-special pricing on their website."

Not acceptable: "Birmingham Buick hides their service prices because they overcharge."

### Rule 4. Pick a recommendation

The page must end with a clear recommendation: "For [target customer description], we recommend [this dealer]." Hedge with the scenarios where the competitor is the better choice (Rule 1), not by refusing to recommend at all.

Buyers who land on the page are trying to make a decision. A page that refuses to recommend wastes their visit.

### Rule 5. Match the dealer's positioning voice

If the dealer's positioning brief specifies a direct, warm, no-pressure voice, the comparison page uses that voice. Comparison pages that adopt a different tone from the rest of the dealer's marketing read as inconsistent.

## The 10-to-15 dimension comparison framework

Every comparison page uses this dimension framework. Some dimensions may not apply to every comparison; skip those rather than padding.

### Foundational dimensions (always include)

**1. Brand portfolio.** Which OEMs each dealer carries new. Used inventory specialization if relevant.

**2. Location and reach.** Address, primary market, surrounding cities served, drive time from key population centers.

**3. History and ownership.** Years in business, ownership type (family, group, public), founding story if newsworthy.

**4. OEM recognition.** Recent and current OEM awards (Mark of Excellence, President's Award, Mark of Quality, Mark of Distinction, etc.). Date specifics where possible.

### Operational dimensions (include where data exists)

**5. Sales department.** Monthly volume signal (if publicly known), brand-portfolio coverage, BDC response time if signaled in reviews, key staff named publicly.

**6. Service department.** Hours, brand-certification status, technician count if publicly known, service capability specifics (alignment racks, paintless dent repair, body shop, EV charging install).

**7. Parts department.** Specialization, OEM parts vs aftermarket policy, hours, online ordering availability.

**8. F&I department.** Lender count if publicly known, programs available, OEM finance programs supported, transparency reputation from reviews.

**9. Bilingual capability.** Spanish-speaking staff across departments. Critical in bilingual markets.

### Customer experience dimensions

**10. Pricing transparency.** Does the dealer publish prices on inventory? Service specials? Are advertised prices on third-party platforms (Cars.com, CarGurus) consistent with on-site pricing?

**11. Review profile.** Google star rating, DealerRater rating, recent review volume and velocity. Both summary and patterns (what customers consistently praise or complain about).

**12. Community involvement.** Sponsorships, charitable programs, local press coverage of community work.

**13. Online presence.** Website usability, mobile experience, online appointment scheduling, online credit application, virtual showroom or video walkarounds.

### Strategic dimensions

**14. Inventory depth.** New vehicle inventory size signal, used inventory size, CPO program participation.

**15. Drive-time recommendation.** For buyers further from one dealer than the other, when does the drive-time tradeoff favor each dealer?

## The comparison page page structure

The page follows this structure. See TEMPLATE.md for the full skeleton.

### Header section
- H1: "[Dealer Name] vs [Competitor Name]: Which [Brand Portfolio] Dealer Is Right for You?"
- Opening 100 words, written verbatim by the skill. Answers the comparison question directly. Picks a recommendation. Acknowledges the scenarios where the competitor wins.

### Quick answer box
- 2 to 3 sentence summary recommendation. Designed for the buyer who reads the first 200 words and decides. Also designed to be the citation AI engines pull.

### Side-by-side comparison table
- 10 to 15 dimensions from the framework above
- For each dimension: this dealer's state, the competitor's state, an honest "advantage" rating (this dealer / competitor / comparable)

### Detailed sections

**"When [This Dealer] is the better choice"** (250 to 400 words)
- Specific buyer scenarios with named customer types
- Specific operational reasons backed by evidence
- 3 to 5 distinct scenarios

**"When [Competitor] is the better choice"** (200 to 300 words)
- Required for credibility (Rule 1)
- Specific scenarios where the competitor wins
- Honest acknowledgment, not perfunctory

**"What we have in common"** (150 to 200 words, optional)
- Dimensions where the dealers are genuinely comparable
- Builds credibility by showing the comparison is fair

### Brand portfolio comparison
- Side-by-side of models each dealer carries
- Especially useful when portfolios differ

### Service department comparison
- Hours, capability, certifications, specifics

### Customer experience comparison
- Pulled from public review patterns
- Frame in terms of what customers consistently say, not the dealer's own self-perception

### Common questions section
- 6 to 10 Q&A on the comparison
- Structured for FAQPage schema
- Questions a real cross-shopping buyer would actually ask

Sample questions:
- "Is [Dealer] or [Competitor] better for service?"
- "Which one has better pricing on [common model]?"
- "Does [Dealer] take trades better than [Competitor]?"
- "Which one is closer to [common reference point]?"
- "Are the F&I products the same?"

### The honest recommendation
- Restates the recommendation from the opening
- Names the specific scenarios where the competitor is the better choice
- Provides a clear next step (schedule a visit, get a pre-qual, etc.)

### CTA section
- Schedule a test drive
- Get pre-approved
- Visit the dealership
- Contact specific staff member by name

## Research methodology for the competitor

Before writing the page, gather public facts about the competitor. The skill produces a quick competitor profile that informs the comparison.

### Sources to check (in priority order)

1. **Competitor's website.** Home page, About page, service page, hours, brand portfolio, key staff, awards displayed.
2. **Competitor's Google Business Profile.** Hours, photos, posts, review patterns.
3. **OEM dealer locator.** For each brand the competitor sells, verify they appear with the same NAP.
4. **Public review data.** Google, DealerRater, Cars.com. Look for patterns, not individual reviews.
5. **Local press.** Recent mentions, awards, community involvement.
6. **OEM press.** Awards and recognition.

When the skill is run in Mode B (with WebSearch or WebFetch), it can fetch these sources directly. In Mode A (without web tools), the skill asks the user to provide a brief profile.

### What to capture in the profile

For each dimension in the framework, capture the competitor's state with citation. Examples:
- Brand portfolio: GMC, Buick (per their home page footer, retrieved [date])
- Hours: Sales Monday-Saturday 8 AM - 8 PM (per their hours page)
- OEM recognition: 2024 Mark of Excellence (per their awards page; not 2025)
- Review profile: 4.4 stars on Google, 1,200+ reviews, common positive theme is staff friendliness, common negative theme is service wait times

### What to never capture

- Inferred or guessed facts ("We think they probably do X")
- Negative characterizations ("They are known for Y")
- Competitor financial information (private, often inaccurate when guessed)
- Speculations about competitor intent or strategy

## How the page gets used

### For AEO/GEO

When a buyer asks ChatGPT, Perplexity, or Gemini "should I go to [Dealer A] or [Dealer B]," AI engines that can find the comparison page will often cite it. Because the page is fair-play, AI engines treat it as a credible source rather than promotional content.

### For Google SEO

The page ranks for "[Dealer A] vs [Dealer B]" and variants because few dealers produce this content. Even modest backlinks and on-page optimization can produce first-page rankings within 60 to 90 days.

### For sales floor enablement

The sales team uses the page (and the accompanying talk track) when customers mention they are cross-shopping. The salesperson can speak to the comparison with respect for the competitor and confidence in the dealer's positioning.

### For BDC enablement

The BDC team can send the comparison page link in follow-up emails when a customer's reply indicates they are also considering the competitor. This is a stronger response than ignoring the cross-shop or pretending it does not exist.

## Updating comparison pages

Comparison pages should be reviewed every 6 months because:
- OEM awards change annually
- Hours and staff change
- Pricing posture can shift
- Review profiles evolve

The review can be light (verify facts, update outdated specifics) or heavier (re-research the competitor, refresh the recommendation if the dealer's own positioning has changed).
